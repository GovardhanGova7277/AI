## Investment Brief – Green Hydrogen in India  

### 1. Executive Summary  
India is positioning itself as a future global hub for green hydrogen, driven by ambitious government missions, a rapidly expanding renewable‑energy base, and strong demand from hard‑to‑decarbonise sectors (steel, fertilizer, transport).  While policy support and abundant solar/wind resources create a compelling case, investors must navigate technical uncertainties, high‑capex electrolyser costs, water‑availability constraints, and a nascent domestic supply chain.  A handful of Indian conglomerates, state‑backed utilities, and foreign strategic partners are already staking early claims in the market.

---

### 2. Policy Landscape & Incentives  

| Policy / Programme | Core Features | Relevance to Investors |
|--------------------|---------------|------------------------|
| **National Green Hydrogen Mission (NGHM)** – launched by the Ministry of New & Renewable Energy (MNRE) | Targets 5 GW of green hydrogen capacity by 2030; provides capital subsidies (up to 30 % of project cost) and viability gap funding for electrolyser projects; earmarks a dedicated “Hydrogen Fund” of INR 10 billion for early‑stage projects. | Reduces upfront CAPEX, improves project economics, and signals long‑term policy certainty. |
| **Renewable Energy Purchase Obligations (REPO) & Renewable Purchase Obligations (RPO) for hydrogen** | Mandates that certain industrial users procure a minimum share of green hydrogen; offers preferential tariffs for renewable electricity used in electrolyser plants. | Guarantees of off‑take and price‑linked electricity lower market risk. |
| **State‑level incentives** (e.g., Gujarat, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu) | Land‑allocation at concessional rates, waiver of stamp duty, and state‑funded grid‑connection subsidies for hydrogen hubs. | Enables clustering of projects and economies of scale. |
| **Tax incentives** – accelerated depreciation on electrolyser assets, GST exemption on hydrogen equipment | Improves internal rate of return (IRR) for investors. | Enhances cash‑flow timing and reduces tax burden. |
| **International collaboration frameworks** – MoUs with Germany, Japan, Australia for technology transfer and co‑financing | Facilitates access to proven electrolyser designs and low‑cost financing. | Lowers technology risk and opens export markets. |

### 3. Economic & Environmental Drivers  

| Driver | Why It Matters | Investor Implication |
|--------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Abundant low‑cost renewable electricity** – India’s solar tariffs have fallen below US$ 0.03 kWh⁻¹ and wind is similarly cheap. | Electricity is the dominant OPEX component of green hydrogen (≈ 70 %). | Low electricity cost can bring green‑hydrogen production cost to **US$ 2–3 kg⁻¹** in optimal sites, making it competitive with grey hydrogen. |
| **Decarbonisation mandates** – India’s Net‑Zero by 2070 pledge, sector‑specific emission targets (steel, fertilizer, shipping). | Creates a regulatory pull for clean fuel and feedstock. | Guarantees future demand and may trigger carbon‑price mechanisms that further improve economics. |
| **Export potential** – Proximity to high‑demand markets (Japan, South Korea, EU) and existing maritime logistics. | Enables revenue diversification beyond domestic off‑take. | Attracts export‑oriented financing and strategic partnerships. |
| **Job creation & industrial upgrading** – Green hydrogen projects are projected to generate 1 million+ direct/indirect jobs by 2035. | Aligns with government’s “Make in India” and employment goals. | May qualify projects for additional fiscal incentives and social‑license benefits. |
| **Water‑energy nexus** – Use of seawater with advanced desalination reduces freshwater stress. | Addresses a key sustainability concern in water‑scarce regions. | Opens financing from ESG‑focused funds that require water stewardship. |

### 4. Technical & Financial Risks  

| Risk Category | Description | Mitigation Strategies |
|---------------|-------------|-----------------------|
| **Electrolyser cost & performance** – Capital cost still US$ 800–1 200 kW⁻¹; efficiency 60‑70 % (PEM) vs 70‑80 % (alkaline). | High upfront CAPEX and lower-than‑expected efficiency can erode margins. | Leverage government subsidies, secure long‑term power purchase agreements (PPAs), and adopt proven OEMs through joint‑venture models. |
| **Water availability & quality** – Large‑scale electrolysis needs 9–12 m³ H₂ per tonne; freshwater scarcity in many states. | Potential bottleneck and added desalination cost. | Co‑locate plants near coastal desalination units; use brackish‑water electrolyser designs; incorporate water‑recycling loops. |
| **Grid integration & curtailment** – Intermittent renewable supply may lead to under‑utilisation of electrolyser capacity. | Reduces capacity factor and revenue. | Deploy hybrid renewable‑plus‑storage (batteries or pumped hydro) to smooth supply; negotiate firm‑capacity contracts with utilities. |
| **Regulatory uncertainty** – Evolving standards for hydrogen safety, transport, and certification. | May delay project approvals and increase compliance costs. | Participate in industry consortia (e.g., Indian Hydrogen Association) to shape standards; adopt internationally recognised safety codes. |
| **Financing gaps** – Limited track record of large‑scale green‑hydrogen projects in India leads to higher perceived risk premiums. | Higher cost of capital (debt > 8 %). | Use blended finance (government grant + commercial debt), green bonds, and multilateral development bank guarantees (e.g., ADB, World Bank). |
| **Supply‑chain immaturity** – Domestic electrolyser manufacturing capacity is nascent; reliance on imports (Germany, Japan). | Exposure to foreign‑exchange risk and lead‑time delays. | Encourage local OEM development through “Make in India” incentives; secure long‑term supply contracts with foreign partners. |

### 6. Investment Outlook  

| Metric | Current Status | Near‑Term Outlook (2025‑2028) |
|--------|----------------|------------------------------|
| **Installed green‑hydrogen capacity** | ~ 0.2 GW (pilot & demonstration) | Expected to reach **2–3 GW** under NGHM subsidies. |
| **Cost trajectory** | US$ 4–5 kg⁻¹ (average) | Target **US$ 2–3 kg⁻¹** for projects with full subsidy and low‑cost solar PPAs. |
| **Financing environment** | Limited pure‑play green‑hydrogen funds; reliance on corporate balance‑sheet. | Growth of dedicated green‑hydrogen funds (e.g., SoftBank‑backed “Hydrogen Fund”) and increased multilateral financing. |
| **Export pipeline** | No commercial export contracts yet. | MoUs with Japan and EU for pilot shipments by 2027. |

Overall, the **investment case is favorable** for capital‑intensive, long‑horizon investors who can absorb early‑stage risk and benefit from policy subsidies.  Projects that combine **renewable‑energy PPAs, water‑resource planning, and secured off‑take (industrial or export)** are likely to achieve the highest IRRs.

---

### 7. Recommendations for Investors  

1. **Target “Hydrogen Hubs”** in states with strong renewable pipelines (Gujarat, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu) to exploit land‑grant and grid‑connection incentives.  
2. **Structure financing with blended capital** – combine government viability‑gap funding, green‑bond issuance, and multilateral guarantees to lower debt cost.  
3. **Secure long‑term offtake contracts** with steel, fertilizer, or shipping firms; consider “of‑ftake‑linked PPAs” that lock in renewable electricity prices.  
4. **Partner with established OEMs** for electrolyser supply to mitigate technology risk and accelerate commissioning.  
5. **Integrate water‑management solutions** (seawater desalination, recycling) from the design stage to avoid future regulatory hurdles.  
6. **Monitor policy evolution** – especially the final NGHM guidelines, carbon‑pricing mechanisms, and any changes to GST or import duties on hydrogen equipment.
